Microphones are transducers capable of transforming acoustic energy into electric energy.
Atmospheric pressure variations are converted into voltage variation, and therefore in an electric current.

There are 2 types of microphones:
- Electrodynamic microphones
	- A coil made of a conducting material is fixed to the diaphragm which receives the sound wave and vibrates as a consequence
	- The coil is located within a magnetic field generated by a magnet inside it
	- When the diaphragm vibrates the coil moves too, generating an oscillation in the magnetic field and thus generating a current in the coil. This results in the electric signal that has been generated having the same rate as the acoustic wave hitting the diaphragm.
	- These microphones are the most resistant and are usually used for live performances
	- The resonance frequency is about 2.5 kH, making them ideal for the correct reproduction of vocals and guitar
	- They are capable of enduring very high sound-pressure

- Condenser
	- Also known as electrostatic microphones, it has a condenser inside it.
	- One of the condenser's two plates is the diaphragm, making it so that the diaphragm vibration produces a variation in the distance between the 2 plates, therefore altering the capacity value.
	- Charge takes time to vary, so the voltage must change instantly
	- In order to polarize the condenser it is necessary to apply some initial voltage, called **phantom power**, and it is usually supplied by the mixer to which the microphone has been connected, or by a battery.
	- These microphones have high sensitivity, which allows them to record sources placed at a distance from them, making them particularly suitable for the use of stereophonic shooting techniques.
	- They also have a very thin diaphragm, which allows good reproduction of higher frequencies, but makes them easily damageable if exposed to very high sound pressures, making them not ideal for live performances but more suitable for recording studio work.

What we call sensitivity, in a microphone, is the ability to accurately convert an acoustic wave-form into an electric signal, which is therefore measured in $\frac{\text{V}}{\text{Pa}}$.
Considering the quantities involved, instead of $\frac{\text{V}}{\text{Pa}}$ we usually use $\frac{\text{mV}}{Pa}$.
Sensitivity is measured by exposing the microphone to a sine wave with a frequency of 1 kHz and an SPL of 98 dB (sound pressure of 1 Pa) at a distance of 1 m (distance is important, since sound pressure decreases linearly with distance).
Another way of expressing the sensitivity value is to use dBV, where the chosen reference is at 1000 $\frac{\text{mV}}{\text{Pa}}$, an unattainable value for any microphone:
$$
\text{Sensitivity}_{\text{dBV}} = 20 \log_{10} \left( \frac{\text{Sensitivity}_{\frac{\text{mV}}{\text{Pa}}}}{1000 \frac{\text{mV}}{\text{Pa}}} \right) \text{dBV}
$$
The sensitivity of a microphone is not constant as the frequency changes so, in order to describe its behavior, a Bode plot in logarithmic scale on the horizontal axis and in dB on the vertical axis is used.
The reference is the sensitivity value at 1 kHz, and the graph always hits 0 dB at 1 kHz, by construction.
Theoretically it tends toward −∞ at 0 Hz and the behavior outside the 20-20000 Hz range is indifferent, because the ear no longer hears.
